# Best Practice Guide: Board Meeting Minutes

General governance best practice for volunteer chapter boards, written by the Isa team in its own words from the public guidance listed under Further reading. It is not ISACA policy. How long you must keep records, and who may see them, depends on your bylaws and local law: ask a person if you need a ruling.

## Why minutes matter

Board meeting minutes are the official record of what the board decided. They let the next meeting pick up where the last one ended, show members and partners how decisions were made, help settle later disagreements about what was agreed, and keep the chapter's memory when leaders change. Because minutes can be used as an official record, they should be accurate, plain and brief.

## What to include in board meeting minutes

Board meeting minutes should include the date, time and place; whether it was a regular or special meeting; who chaired it, which board members attended and who was absent; whether a quorum was present; whether the previous minutes were approved, with any corrections; each motion, who made and seconded it, and the result of the vote; decisions and actions agreed, with who will do them; items held over to a later meeting; and the time the meeting ended. Attach reports instead of copying them into the minutes.

## What to leave out of minutes

Minutes record decisions, not a word-for-word account of the discussion. Leave out personal opinions and remarks about people, arguments between members, praise or criticism, details of legal advice, jargon nobody will understand later, and anything said in confidence. Use names mainly for who made and seconded motions. Summarize the main points of an important debate in a sentence or two.

## Approving, signing and sharing minutes

The secretary drafts the minutes soon after the meeting, checks them against the agenda, and sends the draft to the board so members can suggest corrections. The board approves the minutes, with any corrections, at the start of its next meeting. Once approved, the secretary signs them (some boards also ask the president to sign) and files them. Unsigned or unapproved minutes are only a draft.

## Keeping minutes safe

Keep all approved minutes, with their attachments, in one consistent place that the board can find, with a backup copy. Hand them over to the next secretary and president when leaders change, so the record isn't lost. Rules on how long minutes must be kept differ by country and by your bylaws; many boards simply keep them permanently.

## Common mistakes with minutes

Common mistakes are not recording whether a quorum was present, describing decisions too vaguely to act on, including remarks that could embarrass the chapter, sending minutes out late or approving them late, missing signatures, and filing them in different places so they can't be found later.
