# Best Practice Guide: Committees and Task Forces

General governance best practice for volunteer chapter boards, written by the Isa team in its own words from the public guidance listed under Further reading. It is not ISACA policy: your chapter's bylaws may name committees you must have.

## Standing committees, ad hoc committees and task forces

A standing committee handles ongoing work year after year, such as finance, membership, programs or nominations. An ad hoc committee is set up for one purpose, such as reviewing the bylaws or drafting a strategic plan, and closes when the job is done. A task force is short and focused on one problem, such as planning an event. An advisory group gives expert advice but has no decision-making power. Committees usually make recommendations; the full board makes the decision.

## How many committees a chapter needs

Most boards need fewer standing committees than they think. Review the committees every year against the work the chapter actually needs to do: close committees with no real work, merge committees whose duties overlap, and use short task forces for one-off jobs. Committees are tools for the board's work, not ends in themselves.

## Choosing committee members

Write down what each committee is for and what skills it needs, and share that widely so volunteers can decide whether to join. Match people to committees by their skills, interests and available time, and aim for a mix of experienced members and newer people. Each board member should serve on at least one committee, and usually on no more than two. Committees can include chapter members who are not on the board, which is a good way to spot future board members.

## The committee chair's job

A committee chair turns the board's goals into a work plan and meeting agendas, runs the committee's meetings, follows up with members who miss meetings, makes sure notes are kept, and reports the committee's progress and recommendations to the board. Give every committee a written charge with its goals and deadlines, and review it every year or two.

## Checking how committees are doing

At the end of each year or project, ask whether each committee met its goals, used its members' time well and still needs to exist. Short check-ins at the end of meetings also help. Use what you learn to thank strong contributors and to invite them toward larger leadership roles.
