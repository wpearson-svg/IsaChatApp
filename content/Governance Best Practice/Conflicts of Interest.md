# Best Practice Guide: Conflicts of Interest

General governance best practice for volunteer chapter boards, written by the Isa team in its own words from the public guidance listed under Further reading. It is not ISACA policy, and it is not legal advice: if a conflict involves money, contracts or a dispute, talk to a person.

## What a conflict of interest is

A conflict of interest arises when a board member's personal interest could influence, or look as if it could influence, a decision they take part in. It isn't only about money: a family tie, a job with a supplier or sponsor, or a role in a competing organization can all create a conflict. Conflicts are normal, since board members have lives and careers outside the chapter. What matters is that they are disclosed and handled openly.

## What a conflict of interest policy should include

A conflict of interest policy should say why it exists, ask every board member to agree to follow it, require each board member to fill in a written disclosure of their outside ties every year, keep those disclosures on file and up to date, explain what happens when a member has a conflict on a decision, and expect members to speak up when a new conflict comes up during a discussion.

## What to do when a board member has a conflict in a meeting

When a board member has a conflict of interest on an item, they should declare it before the discussion starts, leave the room for that item and not vote, and the minutes should record that they declared the conflict and did not vote. Some boards let the member answer questions first and then step out. The chair names the conflict and asks the member to step aside. If a member forgets, the chair should give a gentle reminder; if a member disputes the conflict, the chair can pause the item until it is resolved.

## Preventing conflicts

Talk about conflicts of interest when recruiting new board members, include the policy in orientation, and review the disclosures when planning each agenda so the chair can spot conflicts in advance. A board with a range of members is less likely to have one conflict dominate a decision. If a conflict is serious and lasting, the board should discuss whether the member can keep serving in that role.
