# Best Practice Guide: Leadership Succession and Handover

General governance best practice for volunteer chapter boards, written by the Isa team in its own words from the public guidance listed under Further reading. It is not ISACA policy: ISACA's President Guidebook sets out the official turnover steps, and your bylaws set how officers are elected.

## Why plan for succession

Chapter leadership changes regularly, and much of what a leader knows can leave with them. Planning for succession keeps the chapter running smoothly through each change, brings in fresh ideas, and stops leadership resting on the same few people year after year. The best time to start is early in a term, not just before it ends.

## Building a pipeline of future leaders

Treat every active member as a possible future leader. Give promising volunteers growing responsibility, for example a committee role, then a committee chair, then a board seat. Offer training as they go, especially in finance and strategy. A year as treasurer or committee chair is good preparation for president. Ask the whole board what qualities the next leaders need, and invite nominations from every board member.

## Handing over to the next president

Before a term ends, the outgoing president should mentor the incoming president, share the open items and upcoming deadlines, introduce key contacts, and pass on records such as minutes, financial information and passwords, following the chapter's procedures. Writing down what worked, what didn't and what is unfinished saves the next leader months of rediscovery.

## The role of past presidents

Many boards keep the immediate past president on the board for a year after their term. They remember why past decisions were made, can advise the new president, and help keep plans on track. Their role is to support and advise, not to keep leading. Past leaders can also help by recruiting the next group of volunteers.

## Stepping up as a new leader

A new leader should spend the first months learning how the chapter really works: its habits, who holds knowledge and influence, and how decisions get made. Build relationships with fellow board members early, since they drive what the board can achieve. Be honest about your own gaps and ask for help with them. As president, part of the job is to encourage the next group of members to step up.
