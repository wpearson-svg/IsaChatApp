# Best Practice Guide: Orienting New Board Members

General governance best practice for volunteer chapter boards, written by the Isa team in its own words from the public guidance listed under Further reading. It is not ISACA policy: where ISACA's documents or your chapter's bylaws say something different, follow them.

## Why orientation matters

A new board member should have an orientation, even if they have served on other boards before. Every board has its own habits, customs and unwritten rules, and few people pick up governance on their own. A good orientation shortens the learning curve, so new members contribute sooner, and it gives the whole board one shared picture of how it works. It is also the formal start of working relationships between new and returning members.

## What to cover in a new board member orientation

An orientation for new board members should cover the chapter's mission and goals, the key issues the chapter faces right now, what is expected of each board member (time, meetings, committee work), the board meeting calendar, a contact list of the people they will work with, the board handbook, and the minutes of recent board meetings. Leave time at the end for questions. Spend the session on how to be a good board member and how this board works, more than on organizational detail.

## The board handbook

Give every new board member a board handbook, on paper or as a link. A useful handbook holds a welcome letter, the chapter's history, its mission and goals, the bylaws, recent financial information, the strategic plan, a description of each committee and who chairs it, the role of each board member, and key policies such as conflict of interest. Update the handbook before each orientation, so it reflects new programs and policy changes. New members should read it on their own time before the session, so the session can be used for discussion.

## Who should lead the orientation

The chapter president usually leads the orientation, supported by whoever handles board development, such as a governance or nominating committee. Invite returning board members too: they can present their area, act as mentors, and show newcomers the range of people on the board. Some boards bring in a neutral facilitator so that everyone feels free to speak. ISACA's Chapter Experience team can tell you what ISACA provides for new chapter leaders.

## How to run the session

There is no single right format. An orientation can be a few hours or spread over several short sessions, in person or online; a full-day event can overload people. Decide the goals and the agenda in advance. Keep the setting informal, mix short presentations with discussion and questions, and include some social time, since getting to know each other helps the board work as a team. Make clear that the board acts as a group: decisions are made together, not by any one member.

## After the orientation

Orientation is a start, not the whole job. Pair each new board member with an experienced member as a mentor for their first year. Treat their first regular board meeting as the start of their formal duties, and check in after a few months to see what is still unclear. Some boards ask each new member to sign a short statement of what is expected of them and keep it with the board's records.
