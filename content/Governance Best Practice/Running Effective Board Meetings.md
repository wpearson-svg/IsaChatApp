# Best Practice Guide: Running Effective Board Meetings

General governance best practice for volunteer chapter boards, written by the Isa team in its own words from the public guidance listed under Further reading. It is not ISACA policy. ISACA's President Guidebook sets how many board meetings a chapter must hold; your bylaws set notice rules and quorum.

## Planning the board meeting agenda

Start the agenda by deciding what the board must accomplish at this meeting. Carry over unfinished business from the last meeting, ask board members for items and give them time to reply, put the most important items first, and mark which items need a vote. Next to each item, note who will lead it and roughly how long it should take. A common order is: call to order, approval of the last minutes, reports, unfinished business, new business, and adjournment.

## Sending materials before the meeting

Send the agenda and supporting papers well before the meeting, ideally one to two weeks ahead and within any notice period in your bylaws, so members can read them and arrive ready to decide. Short summaries, charts and comparisons with earlier results are easier to read than long reports. Fixing the year's meeting dates in advance helps volunteers keep them free.

## Using a consent agenda

A consent agenda groups routine items, such as approving the last minutes and accepting written reports, so the board can approve them together in one vote without discussion. Any member can ask to move an item off the consent agenda for discussion. This saves meeting time for real decisions and strategy instead of listening to reports read aloud.

## Leading the meeting

The person chairing the meeting, usually the president, should start and end on time, keep to the agenda, and gently stop discussions that wander. Make sure every member gets a chance to speak, draw out quieter members, and welcome different views before a decision. When an item can't be settled, it can be postponed or passed to a committee. If your bylaws adopt a set of meeting rules such as Robert's Rules of Order, the chair should know how motions and votes work under them.

## After the meeting

Within a day or two, send a short note of decisions and action items, with who will do what by when. Carry open action items onto the next agenda as unfinished business. The secretary should send the draft minutes soon after, while memories are fresh. Some boards take five minutes at the end of each meeting to ask what worked and what to change.

## Common board meeting mistakes

The most common mistakes are putting too many items on one agenda, leaving too little time for difficult topics, burying the important items at the end, spending most of the meeting on reports instead of decisions, and ending without anyone assigned to follow up. Long meetings with no decisions are a common reason volunteers lose interest and leave a board.
